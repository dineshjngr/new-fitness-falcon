Creatine for Beginners: Dose, Loading, Side Effects and Myths

Creatine for beginners: how much to take, whether to load, water weight, kidney and hair-loss myths, and how to buy a genuine monohydrate in India.

Creatine for beginners comes down to one habit: take 3–5 grams of plain creatine monohydrate every day, at any time that suits you, and keep lifting. Loading (about 20 grams a day for 5–7 days) fills your muscles faster but is optional. Expect 1–3 kg of early water weight if you load. In healthy adults, creatine has not been shown to damage kidneys, cause baldness or cause cramps. The rest of this guide covers who benefits, how to start, what the side effects really are and how to buy a product you can trust.

Key takeaways

  • Most beginners can take 3–5 grams of plain creatine monohydrate daily, at any convenient time, including rest days.
  • Loading at about 20 grams a day for 5–7 days fills muscles faster but is optional and typically adds 1–3 kg, mostly water.
  • In healthy adults at recommended doses, research has not shown creatine damages kidneys, causes hair loss or causes cramps.
  • People with kidney problems, pregnant or breastfeeding people and under-18s should get medical advice first.

Creatine is one of the most researched sports supplements. The Australian Institute of Sport puts it in Group A, its top evidence tier. It helps you do a little more work in hard sets, and that adds up over months of training.

What is creatine and what does it do?

Creatine is a compound your body makes from amino acids, mainly in the liver and kidneys. You also get it from meat and fish. According to the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand, about 95% of the body's creatine is stored in skeletal muscle, and roughly two-thirds of that is phosphocreatine.

Phosphocreatine is a fast energy buffer. In the first seconds of a heavy set or a sprint, it helps rebuild ATP, the molecule your muscles use for energy. With more phosphocreatine stored, you can often squeeze out an extra rep or two, or recover a bit faster between sprints. A normal diet with 1–2 g of creatine a day leaves muscle stores about 60–80% full. Supplementing can raise muscle creatine and phosphocreatine by roughly 20–40%.

So creatine does not burn fat, and it is not a stimulant, so you will not feel a buzz. If you want an energy kick before training, that is caffeine, which we cover in our guide to pre-workout caffeine dose and timing.

Who benefits most from creatine?

The clearest benefits come from activities built on short, repeated, high-intensity efforts:

  • Resistance training. More reps at a given weight mean more training volume. Over time, that supports strength and muscle gain.
  • Sprint and stop-start sports. Cricket, football, hockey, badminton, kabaddi and similar sports involve repeated bursts with short recoveries.
  • Vegetarians and vegans. Creatine occurs naturally only in animal foods, so plant-based eaters tend to start with lower muscle stores. In an 8-week trial by Burke and colleagues, vegetarians began with lower total muscle creatine than non-vegetarians (117 vs 130 mmol/kg). Vegetarians who took creatine gained more muscle creatine, lean tissue and total work performance than non-vegetarians who took it. That makes creatine especially relevant for many Indian lifters.

The benefit is smaller for steady endurance work such as long, easy runs. Creatine also amplifies training rather than replacing it. If you are new to lifting, a simple plan like our minimum effective strength routine for beginners matters far more than any jar. Women can use the same basic guidance. For female-specific evidence, including studies in postmenopausal women, see our separate article on creatine for women, water weight and safety.

How much creatine should a beginner take?

The ISSN position stand describes two routes to full muscle stores. Both end up in the same place.

Loading vs no loading: two ways to saturate muscle creatine
ApproachDaily amountTime to full storesBest for
Loading, then maintenanceAbout 0.3 g/kg/day (around 20 g, split into four doses) for 5–7 days, then 3–5 g/dayAbout 1 weekSomeone with an event or test coming up soon
Steady daily dose (no loading)3–5 g/day from day oneAbout 3–4 weeksMost beginners: simpler, less stomach upset, less sudden weight change

Worked examples

  • 70 kg lifter who wants to load: 0.3 × 70 = 21 g a day. In practice, take 5 g four times a day (breakfast, lunch, evening snack and dinner) for 5–7 days, then 3–5 g once a day.
  • 55 kg beginner who does not want to load: 3 g a day, every day. Stores fill gradually over about a month.

A 2021 evidence review of common creatine questions concluded that loading is not required, because 3–5 g a day (or about 0.1 g/kg) is effective. If you do load, keep each dose at 10 g or less. The same review notes that single doses above 10 g may cause stomach upset or diarrhoea.

When should you take creatine?

For a beginner, timing matters far less than taking it every day. Creatine works by gradually filling muscle stores, not by acting within the hour. No clearly best time has been shown, so pick the time you will remember.

The ISSN reports that taking creatine with carbohydrate, or with carbohydrate plus protein, more consistently increases how much is retained. It adds that this may not improve performance beyond creatine alone. Stirring your dose into curd, a lassi, a protein shake or a glass of milk with a meal is convenient and fine. Our pre- and post-workout gym diet plan has meal ideas that work well.

What are the real side effects of creatine?

Water weight

This is the most common effect. Creatine draws water into muscle, so the scale often rises early on. The 2021 review notes that a 20 g/day loading phase typically adds 1–3 kg of body mass, mostly water. Several 5–10 week training studies found no lasting rise in total body water. That gain is not fat. If you are tracking body weight during a fat-loss phase, note the date you started and judge your progress by the weekly trend, not one weigh-in.

Stomach upset

This mostly happens with large single doses. Splitting loading doses or skipping loading usually solves it.

What is not a well-supported side effect

The ISSN position stand reports that the peer-reviewed research does not support claims of increased dehydration, cramping, injuries, gastrointestinal upset or kidney dysfunction at recommended doses. It also reports no clinically significant side effects in athletes followed for up to 3 years. If something feels off, stop and get it checked.

Creatine myths: what does the evidence say?

Five common creatine myths checked against the research
MythWhat the evidence shows
"Creatine damages your kidneys"No harm to kidney function has been shown in healthy people at recommended doses. A 2026 meta-analysis of randomized trials found a small rise in blood creatinine (about 0.13 mg/dL) but no difference in urea or estimated GFR. The authors called for trials longer than a year.
"Creatine makes you go bald"This idea comes from a single small study that measured a hormone, not hair. A 2025 randomized trial that measured hair directly found no difference from placebo (details below).
"It dehydrates you and causes cramps"Both major reviews report no evidence that creatine causes dehydration or cramping. Drink normally. Our daily water intake guide covers hot-weather training.
"You need to cycle off it"There is no established need to cycle. The ISSN notes that when you stop, stores return to baseline over about 4–6 weeks and do not fall below it. Long-term suppression of your body's own production does not appear to occur.
"Creatine is a steroid"No. Creatine has a completely different chemical structure from anabolic steroids, which are synthetic versions of testosterone. It is also found naturally in everyday foods such as fish and meat.

The hair loss question in detail

In a 2009 study of 20 college rugby players, levels of DHT (a hormone linked to pattern baldness) rose 56% after a week of loading and stayed 40% above baseline after two weeks of maintenance. That study never measured hair, and the DHT rise was not seen in the larger, longer trial below.

In a 2025 randomized controlled trial, resistance-trained men took 5 g of creatine or a placebo daily for 12 weeks. Thirty-eight finished the study. Researchers found no differences in DHT, the DHT-to-testosterone ratio, hair density, follicle counts or hair thickness. The trial had limits: it lasted only 12 weeks, included only men, and some authors are affiliated with the ISSN. On the current evidence, creatine does not appear to cause hair loss.

Creatine vs creatinine on blood tests

Your body breaks creatine down into creatinine, which doctors use as a marker of kidney function. Supplementing (or eating a lot of meat) can raise blood creatinine without any kidney damage, which can make an eGFR estimate look worse than your kidneys actually are. The 2021 review cites an earlier analysis of creatine studies: 12 showed no rise in serum creatinine, 8 showed a rise that stayed within the normal range, and only 2 showed a rise above normal limits. Before any blood test, tell your doctor you take creatine so they can interpret the result correctly. They may suggest a different kidney test if needed.

Which form of creatine should you buy?

Buy creatine monohydrate. It is the form used in almost all the research, and nearly all of it is absorbed. The 2021 review found no peer-reviewed evidence that equal amounts of other forms increase muscle creatine more than monohydrate. That covers creatine salts (HCl is one), ethyl ester, buffered creatine, nitrate and liquid creatine. Some of these forms performed worse. "Micronised" monohydrate is still monohydrate. It just mixes more easily, which is a convenience, not a performance upgrade.

Buying checklist

  1. Label says "creatine monohydrate" with grams per serving clearly stated, and no proprietary blend.
  2. Unflavoured, single ingredient. It is usually cheaper per gram, and you know exactly what you are taking.
  3. Third-party testing. If you compete in a drug-tested sport, choose a product certified by a program such as Informed Sport or NSF Certified for Sport. Check the exact product and batch on the certifier's own website, not just a logo on the tub.
  4. In India, buy from the brand's own site or authorised sellers. Counterfeit supplements are a known problem on marketplaces. Check for a valid FSSAI licence number on the label and an intact seal. Use the brand's authenticity check (scratch code or QR) if it offers one.
  5. Be wary of unusually low prices. Pure monohydrate is a fine, white, nearly tasteless powder; a strong smell or sweetness in an "unflavoured" tub is a red flag.
  6. Skip "creatine plus" stacks for a first purchase. If a pre-workout already contains creatine, count it towards your daily grams.

Who should avoid creatine or check with a doctor first?

  • People with kidney disease or reduced kidney function, or anyone taking medicines that affect the kidneys. The ISSN itself advises a physician consultation "in an abundance of caution."
  • Pregnant or breastfeeding people. Research is still limited, so get individual advice.
  • Under-18s. The ISSN considers supervised use acceptable for adolescent athletes in structured training who eat a balanced diet and stick to recommended doses. A teenager should involve a parent and a doctor or qualified coach, not decide alone.

How to start creatine this week: a step-by-step plan

  1. Day 1: buy unflavoured creatine monohydrate using the checklist above. Get a cheap digital kitchen scale if your scoop size is unclear.
  2. Day 1: log a baseline. Note your body weight and your best sets on two or three lifts, for example 3 × 8 at a given weight.
  3. Choose your route. Most beginners should take 3–5 g once a day. Load (around 20 g a day in four doses for 5–7 days) only if you want faster saturation and do not mind the extra water weight.
  4. Attach it to an existing habit, such as breakfast, your post-workout shake or dinner. Take it on rest days too.
  5. Keep training progressively. Creatine only helps if you use the extra capacity. Our progressive overload guide shows how to add reps and weight week by week.
  6. Review at week 4–6. Compare your lift log, not just the scale. If you notice nothing, that can happen. Creatine responses vary, especially if you already eat a lot of meat or fish.

Common questions

Do I need to take creatine on rest days?

Yes. The goal is to keep muscle stores topped up, which depends on daily use, not on taking it around workouts. Take your usual 3–5 g on rest days too.

How long until creatine works?

With loading, muscle stores are close to full in about a week. With 3–5 g a day, it takes roughly 3–4 weeks. Any performance difference shows up as slightly better training over the following weeks and months, not overnight.

Will I lose my gains if I stop taking creatine?

Muscle creatine drifts back to baseline over about 4–6 weeks, and some water weight goes with it. Strength and muscle you built through training do not disappear because you stopped a supplement.

Is creatine safe for women?

The same basic dosing and safety evidence applies, and studies in women have not found a pattern of serious adverse effects. For female-specific research, see our creatine for women guide.

Sources and evidence notes

Evidence checked on October 11, 2026. This article is an editorial summary of the sources below, not a new systematic review. Example doses and the week-one plan are our practical suggestions for healthy adults based on those sources, not individual medical advice. Several reviews are by ISSN-affiliated researchers; we note limitations where relevant.

  1. ISSN position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation (Kreider et al., 2017): loading and maintenance doses, time to saturation, washout, carbohydrate co-ingestion, safety and the renal caution.
  2. Common questions and misconceptions about creatine supplementation (Antonio et al., 2021): water retention, steroid, kidney, cramping, loading and forms; an expert review, not a meta-analysis.
  3. Creatine supplementation and kidney function: meta-analysis of randomized trials (Tsiaras et al., 2026): small serum creatinine rise with no difference in urea or eGFR; authors call for trials longer than 1 year.
  4. Creatine and the DHT:testosterone ratio in rugby players (van der Merwe et al., 2009): n = 20; measured hormones, not hair.
  5. Does creatine cause hair loss? A 12-week randomized controlled trial (Lak et al., 2025): 38 men completed the trial; no hormone or hair differences; short, male-only.
  6. Creatine and weight training in vegetarians (Burke et al., 2003): lower baseline muscle creatine and larger responses in vegetarians; small sample.
  7. Australian Institute of Sport: Group A supplements: creatine monohydrate listed among supplements with strong evidence for use in specific sport situations.
