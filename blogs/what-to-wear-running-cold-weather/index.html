What to Wear Running in Cold Weather: A Temperature-by-Temperature Guide

What to wear running in cold weather, from the 50s to below 10°F: layers by temperature, wind chill and frostbite times, and when to move your run indoors.

The short answer: dress as if it's about 20°F warmer than the thermometer says. Sports medicine physicians and university running specialists give this rule. You should feel a little chilly for the first few minutes, because running heats you up fast. Wear a thin moisture-wicking layer next to your skin, not cotton. As the temperature drops, add an insulating layer and then a wind-resistant shell. Protect your hands, ears and face early, because they get cold first.

Key takeaways

  • Dress as if it's about 20°F warmer than the thermometer reads, the rule sports medicine physicians at the University of Kansas Health System and MU Health Care give. Expect to feel a little cold for the first few minutes.
  • Layer from the skin out: a moisture-wicking base layer (not cotton), an insulating middle layer as it gets colder, and a wind-resistant shell in the teens and below.
  • Check the wind chill as well as the temperature. On the National Weather Service chart, 0°F with a 15 mph wind feels like −19°F, and exposed skin can get frostbite in 30 minutes.
  • Redness or pain can be the first sign of frostbite. Shivering, confusion, fumbling hands and slurred speech are warning signs of hypothermia, which can happen even above 40°F if you're wet from sweat or rain.
  • Move indoors when the wind chill nears −18°F, the paths are icy, or freezing rain is falling. If you have a heart or lung condition or Raynaud's, check with your doctor first.

The 20-degree rule, and why you should start cold

Running produces a lot of body heat, so clothes that feel right at your front door will feel too warm 10 minutes later. Dr. David Smith of the University of Kansas Health System puts it this way: dress "as if it were 20 degrees warmer than the true outdoor temperature." He also advises layering "but do not overdo it." Dr. Aaron Gray, a sports medicine physician at MU Health Care, gives the same rule, and so do Drexel's running specialists, who add that you should expect to feel a bit cold at the start.

Overdressing isn't just uncomfortable. Extra layers make you sweat, and wet clothing pulls heat away once you slow down or the wind picks up. The CDC notes that hypothermia can occur even at cool temperatures above 40°F (4°C) if a person is chilled by rain, sweat or cold water.

The rows for the 30s, 20s and 10s follow the examples Drexel's Running Performance and Research Center published for 30, 20 and 10 degrees. At zero degrees, its advice is simply "Run inside!" The 40s and 50s rows apply the same 20-degree rule: a 50°F run feels like a 70°F one once you're moving. Nobody tested these outfits for this article. Use them as a first guess and adjust after a run or two.

A few adjustments help:

  • Wind: Dress as if it were one row colder, or add a wind-resistant outer layer. Dr. Gray notes that cold wind on sweaty skin quickly lowers skin temperature. He suggests windproof clothing or routes with natural windbreaks.
  • Sun: According to the National Weather Service (NWS), bright sunshine can raise the wind chill by 10°F to 18°F, so a sunny day can feel a row warmer.
  • Rain or wet snow: Wet clothing speeds heat loss. Choose an outer layer that sheds water, and keep the run shorter if you can't stay dry.
  • Pace: Hard workouts make more heat than easy runs. For an easy run, a long run or a run with walk breaks, dress a little warmer.
  • You: Some people run cold. Note what you wore and how it felt, and you'll soon have your own version of this table.

How to layer: base, insulation, shell

Layers let you match your clothing to the conditions, and you can take one off during the run. The NWS advises dressing in layers of loose-fitting, lightweight, warm clothing, and the CDC's National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) points out that tight clothing reduces blood flow to the hands and feet. For running, each layer has its own job:

  • Base layer, to move sweat: A thin, moisture-wicking fabric next to your skin. Dr. Smith recommends a wicking base layer rather than an absorbent material like cotton. Cotton soaks up sweat and stays wet, which is exactly what you don't want once you slow down.
  • Middle layer, to insulate: A long-sleeve shirt or light fleece in the 30s, and a thicker fleece in the 20s and below. Skip it on milder days.
  • Outer layer, to block wind and wet: A wind-resistant jacket, plus wind-resistant pants in the teens and below. Drexel's specialists suggest zippered pieces, so you can let heat out without stopping.

The CDC lists the nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingers and toes as the areas frostbite most often affects. The NWS advises that mittens are better than gloves, which is why the colder rows of the table switch to mittens. Choose socks that keep your feet warm without squeezing them. Dr. Smith also notes that cold can stiffen the rubber soles of running shoes. If you're buying a new pair before winter, our guide to choosing running shoes for flat feet covers fit and support.

Wind chill: check it before every winter run

The NWS wind chill index estimates how cold it feels on exposed skin when wind speeds up heat loss. It applies when the temperature is at or below 50°F and the wind is faster than 3 mph. A few readings from the official NWS wind chill chart:

  • 30°F with a 10 mph wind feels like 21°F.
  • 20°F with a 15 mph wind feels like 6°F.
  • 0°F with a 15 mph wind feels like −19°F. At that wind chill, exposed skin can get frostbite in 30 minutes.

At colder or windier combinations, the chart's frostbite time drops to 10 minutes and then to 5 minutes. The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position stand on preventing cold injuries during exercise recommends using the wind chill index to estimate frostbite risk. It calls for closer monitoring of exercisers when the wind chill falls below −18°F (−27°C).

The NWS also clears up a common myth: frostbite needs the actual air temperature to be below freezing, so wind alone can't freeze your skin on a 40°F day. Hypothermia is still possible at those temperatures, though. Check the wind chill along with the temperature. On a windy day, consider heading out into the wind and coming home with it at your back. That way you aren't facing a cold headwind at the end, when you're sweaty and tired.

Cold-weather running safety

Know the warning signs of frostbite

According to the CDC, redness or pain in any skin area may be the first sign of frostbite. Later signs include a white or grayish-yellow patch of skin, skin that feels unusually firm or waxy, and numbness. Because the area goes numb, people often don't notice frostbite until someone else points it out. If you see these signs, get indoors and get medical care. Don't rub or massage the area, and don't warm it with a heating pad, stove or radiator, because numb skin burns easily.

Know the warning signs of hypothermia

The CDC lists the warning signs of hypothermia in adults as shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness. NIOSH lists the late signs as no shivering, blue skin, dilated pupils, slowed pulse and breathing, and loss of consciousness. A body temperature below 95°F (35°C) is a medical emergency. If a running partner becomes clumsy or confused, stop the run and get them somewhere warm. Remove any wet clothing and get medical help.

Be seen in the shorter daylight

Daylight saving time ends on Sunday, November 1, 2026, which pushes after-work runs into dusk or darkness. Both the Kansas and Drexel tips recommend reflective clothing. Add a light and choose well-lit routes. Our guide to keeping your workouts and sleep on track when daylight saving time ends covers the time change itself.

Respect ice

Drexel's running specialists note that snow is generally manageable, but ice is not. Slow down, stick to cleared routes, and be careful on shaded patches. Dr. Smith notes that some runners wear traction straps on snow and ice, and Drexel adds that trail-running shoes can help in snow. If the roads are glazed, run indoors.

Warm up, hydrate and change quickly

  • Warm up: Drexel's specialists suggest starting slowly and extending your warm-up so your muscles can adjust. The Kansas tips also stress warming up and cooling down.
  • Drink water: Drexel advises drinking water even though you may not feel thirsty in the cold. Our article on how much water you really need each day covers everyday fluid needs.
  • Change into dry clothes: Once you're inside, change right away. The NWS and Drexel both recommend it, because damp layers chill you quickly after you stop.

Cold air, asthma and exercise-induced bronchoconstriction

In exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB), the airways narrow during or after exercise. The American Thoracic Society notes that EIB occurs in a substantial proportion of people with asthma and can also occur in people without known asthma. The American College of Allergy, Asthma and Immunology (ACAAI) explains that cold air usually holds little moisture, and breathing dry air quickly dehydrates the airways and makes them narrow. The dryness, more than the temperature, is the likely trigger. ACAAI suggests covering your mouth and nose with a scarf or face mask in cold weather and warming up gently for about 15 minutes. If you cough, wheeze or feel tight in the chest on winter runs, talk to your doctor or an allergist rather than pushing through.

Heart conditions, Raynaud's and other health issues

ACSM's position stand says people with asthma and cardiovascular disease can exercise in the cold but should be monitored closely. If you have heart disease, high blood pressure, diabetes or circulation problems, ask your doctor about winter running and any limits you should follow. The same goes for Raynaud's phenomenon. The National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases (NIAMS) explains that in Raynaud's, blood vessels in the fingers and toes narrow, and cold is the most common trigger. The CDC also lists poor blood circulation as a frostbite risk factor.

When to move your run indoors

No single cutoff temperature suits everyone. ACSM's position is that exercise can be done safely in most cold-weather environments with the right precautions. Still, consider an indoor session in any of these situations:

  • The wind chill is around −18°F (−27°C) or colder. That's ACSM's threshold for closer monitoring, and it's close to the NWS line for frostbite in 30 minutes.
  • Roads or paths are icy.
  • It's raining near freezing and you can't stay dry.
  • You're new to winter running and the temperature is near 0°F (−18°C), where Drexel's specialists say to run inside.

A treadmill is the closest substitute. If you're thinking of buying one, our walking pad vs treadmill comparison explains why you need a full treadmill for running. Without one, our indoor workouts for bad air quality days work just as well on icy ones, and so do our home workouts that actually work.

FAQ

What should I wear running in 40-degree weather?

Under the 20-degree rule, a 40°F (4°C) run feels like about 60°F once you're warm. A long-sleeve wicking shirt with shorts or light tights, light gloves and a thin hat or headband is a good starting point. Add a light jacket or vest if it's windy or wet.

What temperature is too cold to run outside?

There's no universal limit. It depends on wind, ice, your health and your experience. For reference, the NWS chart shows frostbite on exposed skin in 30 minutes at a −19°F wind chill. ACSM recommends closer monitoring below a −18°F wind chill, and Drexel's running specialists suggest moving inside at about 0°F.

Are mittens or gloves better for winter running?

The NWS advises that mittens are better than gloves in extreme cold. Light gloves are usually enough in the 30s and 40s. Switch to mittens in the 20s and below, or whenever your fingers start going numb.

Is breathing cold air bad for your lungs when running?

It can be a problem if you're prone to exercise-induced bronchoconstriction. According to ACAAI, breathing cold, dry air quickly can dry out and narrow the airways. ACAAI suggests covering your mouth and nose with a scarf or face mask and warming up first. If you have symptoms, see a doctor.

This article is general information, not medical advice. The clothing table is a starting point based on the sources below, and no gear was tested for this article. If you have a heart or lung condition, Raynaud's or another health concern, talk to your doctor before running in the cold.
