What Is a Readiness Score? Should You Skip Your Workout When It’s Low?

By Dinesh September 30, 2026 4 min read 0 Comments

Quick answer

A readiness score condenses recent recovery signals into a daily suggestion. A low number is a reason to check sleep, symptoms and your planned training load; it is not an automatic instruction to skip every workout. Easy movement may still be appropriate, while fever, chest symptoms or significant illness call for a different decision.

The scores are not interchangeable

Apple’s Series 12 and Ultra 4 feature uses a 0–10 scale from recent activity, vitals and sleep score, and updates during the day. Garmin Training Readiness uses a 1–100 score with sleep, recovery time, HRV status, acute load and recent stress and sleep history on supported watches. WHOOP calls its metric Recovery and includes overnight HRV, resting heart rate, sleep and other signals. Oura’s Readiness Score uses nine contributors, including sleep, HRV balance, resting heart rate, temperature and activity. Their baselines and algorithms differ, so an Oura 65 is not equivalent to a Garmin 65.

A three-step workout decision

First ask whether you feel ill or have symptoms that warrant medical care. A watch cannot clear you to train through them. Second, look at the contributing signals: one restless night is different from several days of poor sleep with rising resting heart rate. Third, match the response to the session. Keep an easy walk, shorten a moderate session or postpone maximal intervals when several warning signs align. Example: your score is low after a late flight, but you feel fine. An easy 20-minute walk and a normal meal may be more useful than treating the number as a ban on movement. If you are unusually exhausted during the warm-up, stop or scale back.

Use trends, not obedience

A baseline takes time to establish, and missing or noisy sensor data can alter the result. Compare the score with your own performance and how you feel. If you repeatedly perform well on “low” days, review how you wear the device and whether its inputs capture your actual training.

Match the response to the evidence

What you noticeReasonable next step
Low score, normal energy, easy session plannedTrain easily; reassess during the warm-up
Low score plus poor sleep and heavy legsShorten or lower the intensity
Low score plus fever, chest symptoms or marked illnessDo not use the score to clear exercise; seek appropriate medical advice
High score but pain or unusual exhaustionRespect symptoms even though the app is optimistic

These are practical examples, not medical rules or brand-specific score thresholds. A score is more useful when it changes one decision you can explain: “I moved intervals because sleep and warm-up performance were both poor.”

How the brands build different answers

Apple updates its 0–10 readiness estimate through the day as activity and vitals change. Garmin’s Training Readiness also updates through the day and includes recent training load and recovery time. WHOOP’s Recovery is calculated around sleep and compares overnight physiological signals with a personal baseline. Oura weighs multiple recent and longer-term contributors, including sleep regularity and activity balance. The timing alone can make the numbers disagree.

Look at the contributor view before acting. If Oura is reacting to several irregular nights, a single good night may not fully reset its score. If Garmin is reacting to acute load after a hard run, an easy bike ride may still fit the training plan. If Apple changes after a midday workout, the new score reflects a different moment than the morning reading.

Build a decision habit without overreacting

For two to four weeks, note your planned session, readiness category, sleep, perceived energy and whether you completed the workout as intended. You do not need a complicated spreadsheet. A brief note after the warm-up often tells you whether the score added anything beyond what you already felt.

If you repeatedly skip training on low-score days but feel fine and make no progress, the score may be too influential in your routine. If you repeatedly push through several low readings and feel worse, use the pattern as a prompt to review workload and recovery. The goal is a sustainable plan, not a perfect daily number.

When a low score is genuinely useful

Readiness can catch a pattern that is easy to dismiss: three short nights, a harder-than-usual training week and a rising resting heart rate. A busy person may feel “fine enough” while overlooking accumulated fatigue. The score gives them a reason to review the week and protect the next important session by making today easier.

It can also be misleading after a missed night of wear, a loose sensor or a training session logged elsewhere. Ask whether the underlying data are complete before treating the label as advice. The best use is to make a specific, reversible adjustment—such as reducing today’s intensity—then watch whether sleep and performance improve. No current consumer score can tell you with certainty that an injury will occur or that you are free of illness.
