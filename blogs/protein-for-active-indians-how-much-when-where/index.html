Protein for Active Indians: How Much You Need, When to Eat It, and Where to Find It

How much protein active Indians need (0.83 to 2.0 g/kg), how to time it across meals, and protein-rich Indian foods from dal and paneer to eggs and soya.

If you train regularly, whether that means morning runs, gym sessions, yoga or weekend cricket, you have probably heard that protein matters. What is less clear is how much you actually need, whether timing makes a difference, and how to get enough from a typical Indian diet without overthinking every meal. This guide breaks it down in plain language, using published nutrition guidance as the base.

Key takeaways

  • ICMR-NIN (2020) sets a reference intake of about 0.83 g of protein per kg of body weight per day for healthy adults.
  • Regular exercisers often benefit from 1.4 to 2.0 g/kg per day (ISSN); strength gains tend to level off around 1.6 g/kg.
  • Total daily intake matters more than timing, but 20 to 40 g per meal spread every three to four hours is a practical pattern.
  • Dal, paneer, curd, soya, eggs, chicken and fish can cover most needs; pairing cereals with pulses improves plant protein quality.
  • Protein powder can top up busy days, but whole foods should make up most of your intake.

Why Protein Matters When You Exercise

Protein is made up of amino acids, the building blocks your body uses to build and maintain muscle, tendons, skin, enzymes and much more. (For a fuller primer, see what protein is and the different types.) Exercise, especially strength training, creates small amounts of wear in muscle tissue. Over the hours and days that follow, your body repairs and adapts, and it needs a steady supply of amino acids to do that well.

Protein also tends to be filling. Many people find that protein-rich meals keep them satisfied for longer.

A few points worth remembering:

  • Protein works alongside training, sleep and total food intake. It is one part of the picture, not a shortcut.
  • Carbohydrates are still your main fuel for hard sessions, so protein should complement them rather than replace them.
  • Most people do best with a consistent daily routine rather than a few very high-protein meals.

How Much Protein Do You Actually Need?

For healthy adults, the ICMR-NIN Nutrient Requirements for Indians (2020) set a reference intake of about 0.83 g of protein per kilogram of body weight per day. That figure is meant to cover the needs of most of the general population.

People who exercise regularly often benefit from more. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand on protein and exercise suggests that 1.4 to 2.0 g per kilogram per day is sufficient for most exercising individuals. A widely cited 2018 meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine found that the benefit of extra protein for strength-training gains tended to level off at around 1.6 g per kilogram per day.

Here is what that looks like for a 65 kg person:

  • General reference intake (0.83 g/kg): about 54 g per day
  • Regular exerciser, lower end (1.4 g/kg): about 91 g per day
  • Regular exerciser, higher end (1.6 g/kg): about 104 g per day

These are ranges, not targets you have to hit to the gram. Your age, training load and overall diet all play a part, and our guide to how much protein you need per day by weight, age and goal works through more examples. If you have a health condition, are pregnant, or are unsure what suits you, a registered dietitian or your doctor can help you set a number that fits.

Does Timing Matter?

Timing is less important than your total daily intake, but how you spread protein through the day can still help. The ISSN position stand notes that doses of roughly 20 to 40 g of protein per meal, spread every three to four hours, are a practical way to support muscle repair across the day.

In practice, this means:

  1. Spread it out. Many Indian diets are light on protein at breakfast and heavier at dinner. Moving some protein to breakfast and lunch evens things out.
  2. Eat a balanced meal after training. A meal or snack with both protein and carbohydrates within a couple of hours of your session is a sensible habit. The so-called "anabolic window" is wider than gym myths suggest, so there is no need to panic if you eat a little later. If you are weighing up morning versus evening intake, see the best time to consume protein.

Good Sources of Protein in an Indian Diet

The good news is that Indian kitchens already have plenty of protein-rich staples. The values below are approximate and vary by brand, recipe and portion size.

Vegetarian options

  • Paneer (100 g): about 18 g
  • Hung curd or Greek-style yoghurt (100 g): about 8 to 10 g
  • Milk (1 glass, 250 ml): about 8 g
  • Cooked dal (1 katori, about 150 g): about 7 to 9 g
  • Rajma or chole, cooked (1 katori): about 7 to 9 g
  • Soya chunks (50 g dry): about 25 g
  • Roasted chana (30 g): about 5 to 6 g
  • Peanuts (30 g): about 7 g
  • Tofu (100 g): about 8 to 12 g, depending on firmness

For more ideas, see our list of high-protein vegetarian foods.

Non-vegetarian options

  • Egg (1 large): about 6 g
  • Chicken breast, cooked (100 g): about 28 to 31 g
  • Fish such as rohu or surmai, cooked (100 g): about 20 to 22 g

A note on plant protein quality

Most plant foods are lower in one or more essential amino acids on their own. The classic Indian pairing of dal with rice or roti helps here, because cereals and pulses complement each other. The ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024) recommend combining cereals and pulses for this reason. If you eat mostly vegetarian, aim for variety across dals, dairy, soya, nuts and seeds through the week. Our guide to high-protein plant foods covers more options.

A Sample High-Protein Day

Below is an example day for someone aiming for roughly 90 to 100 g of protein. Adjust portions to your own needs and appetite. If you want a fully vegetarian version built without supplements, try our 100 g protein Indian vegetarian meal plan.

  • Breakfast: 2 moong dal chillas with paneer filling and a bowl of curd
  • Mid-morning: a handful of roasted chana and a fruit
  • Lunch: 2 rotis, a katori of rajma, mixed vegetable sabzi and salad
  • Post-workout: a glass of milk or a smoothie made with curd and banana
  • Dinner: grilled chicken or tofu tikka, a katori of dal, rice and sautéed vegetables

On days when a busy schedule makes a full meal hard to manage, some people find it convenient to top up with a protein powder. If you go this route, choose a product with clear labelling and follow the serving directions on the pack. For example, Horlicks Protein Powder can be mixed into milk as a quick addition to an otherwise balanced diet. Whole foods should still make up most of your intake.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Back-loading protein into dinner. A light breakfast and a heavy dinner leaves long gaps. Spreading intake is usually more practical.
  • Ignoring total calories. If you eat too little overall, your body has less to work with for recovery, no matter how much protein you eat.
  • Relying only on supplements. Powders are convenient, but whole foods bring fibre, vitamins and minerals that a shake cannot fully replace.
  • Copying someone else's numbers. An athlete's target may not suit a beginner, and the reverse is also true.

The Bottom Line

You do not need an extreme diet to meet your protein needs as an active person. Know your rough daily range, spread protein across your meals, lean on familiar Indian staples like dal, paneer, curd, eggs and soya, and keep the rest of your diet balanced. Consistency over weeks and months matters far more than any single meal.
