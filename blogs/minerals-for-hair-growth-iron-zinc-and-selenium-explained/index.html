Iron, Zinc and Selenium for Hair: Deficiency, Supplements and Risks

Understand why hair supplements are not universal treatments, when deficiencies may need assessment, and the risks of excess iron, zinc and selenium.

Iron, zinc, and selenium are essential nutrients, but a hair supplement is not a diagnosis or a universal treatment. Hair shedding, reduced density, and breakage can have different causes.

Start with the cause of the change

The American Academy of Dermatology recommends assessment of the cause of hair loss. A clinician may investigate a suspected deficiency, but supplements are not automatically useful when nutrient levels are normal. Excess iron can be harmful.

Iron: essential, but more is not always better

According to the NIH iron factsheet, iron helps make the proteins that carry oxygen in the blood and muscles. Foods include meat, lentils, beans, and fortified cereals. Vitamin C-containing foods can improve absorption of plant-source iron. Iron supplements can cause adverse effects and interact with medicines; keep them away from children and use them only as advised.

Zinc: do not treat a label as a treatment plan

The NIH zinc factsheet describes zinc’s roles in immune function and other normal body processes. High intakes can interfere with copper status and medicines. A product marketed for hair growth is not evidence that you need extra zinc.

Selenium: excess can affect hair

The NIH selenium factsheet warns that excessive intake can cause hair and nail loss. Combining several supplements can unintentionally increase total intake. Do not use a high-dose product to treat unexplained shedding.

A useful appointment checklist

  • Note when the change began and whether you see shedding, broken ends, a widening part, or patches.
  • Bring a list or photos of medicines and supplement labels, including doses.
  • Describe recent changes in diet, illness, or hair styling.
  • Ask what cause is suspected, whether testing is needed, and how progress should be assessed.

This checklist organises a conversation; it does not identify the cause for you. If a supplement is recommended, ask how long to take it and whether follow-up testing is needed.

Editorial correction

Updated 6 October 2026: removed claims that iron, zinc, and selenium are universally the most important hair-growth treatment. The article now distinguishes nutritional needs from diagnosis and explains excess-intake risks.
